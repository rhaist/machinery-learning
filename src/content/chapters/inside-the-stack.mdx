A model is one two-part step (a *layer*) stacked dozens of times, 30 to over 120 layers in today's large models:

- **Look around** (attention, the previous chapter): each word gathers context from earlier words.
- **Think** (engineers call this the MLP or feed-forward layer): each word, on its own, works through what it now knows. Much of the model's stored knowledge seems to live here.

Each layer refines the understanding a little further. Interpretability studies suggest that, roughly, early layers sort out words and grammar, middle layers work with ideas and relationships ("this is a bug report", "the user is frustrated"), and late layers turn all of that into a concrete guess about the next piece of text. At the very end, the model produces a score for every possible next token.

## What "70 billion parameters" means

The <Term k="params">parameters</Term> are the model's dials: the numbers inside all those layers that training adjusted, one tiny nudge at a time. "A 70B model" has 70 billion of them. They encode everything the model learned. The training data stops at some date, and the dials don't change afterwards. That's why models have a knowledge cutoff.

## Mixture of experts: running only part of the model

Bigger models know more, but running every dial for every word is expensive. So most leading open-weight models, and at least some closed ones such as Google's Gemini, now use a <Term k="moe">Mixture of Experts</Term>: the "think" part is split into dozens or hundreds of specialists, and a small router picks a handful of them for each word, so only about 5% of the model works on any one word. <Claim id="deepseek-moe" cap />.

<Note title="Like a large consultancy">
The firm employs hundreds of experts, but each question only goes to a few of them, chosen by a router. The firm's total knowledge is huge; the cost of answering any single question stays modest. Unlike a real firm, the "experts" aren't the tax person or the lawyer: their specialisms are statistical, and nobody designed them.
</Note>

<Engineer title="Residual stream, superposition and reading a model card">
Every layer reads the same running vector for each token and adds its contribution to it: x = x + layer(x). Engineers call this the <Term k="residual">residual stream</Term>. It works like a shared bus that every component reads from and writes to.

After the last layer, the final position's vector is multiplied by the unembedding matrix to give the <Term k="logits">logits</Term>, one score per vocabulary token. Most parameters sit in the <Term k="mlp">MLPs</Term>: about 80% in a dense model like Llama 3 70B, and more in MoE models. Most of the rest are in the attention projections.

**More concepts than dimensions.** A residual stream 8,192 numbers wide can hold far more than 8,192 concepts. The model packs them into directions that are almost, but not quite, at right angles, and accepts a little interference between them. This is <Term k="superposition">superposition</Term>, shown here in two dimensions:

<Superposition />

Superposition is why a single neuron usually responds to several unrelated things. To pull the concepts apart, researchers train sparse autoencoders that split the vectors into millions of features, each of which fires rarely and often has a readable meaning.

**Reading a model card.** A typical spec line decodes like this:

| Spec | Meaning |
|---|---|
| 70B parameters, 80 layers | 80 blocks of attention plus MLP |
| Hidden size 8192 | The residual stream is 8,192 numbers wide |
| 64 attention heads, 8 KV heads | 64 query heads share 8 sets of keys and values (<Term k="gqa">GQA</Term>), which shrinks the <Term k="kv">KV cache</Term> |
| 128k context | The window, in tokens. Llama 3.1 introduced it; the first Llama 3 had 8k |
| RoPE, SwiGLU, RMSNorm | Rotary positions, the MLP's gate function and the normalisation: standard choices |

**MoE in practice.** The router is a small learned layer that scores every expert for each token, and usually the top 2 to 8 run. Early designs such as Mixtral used 2 of 8 large experts. Current ones use many small experts, so DeepSeek-V3, Llama 4 Maverick and Qwen3.5 activate only about 4–6% of their parameters per token. All experts must still sit in memory, so MoE saves compute, not RAM. An extra term in the training loss keeps the router from sending everything to a few favourites.
</Engineer>
