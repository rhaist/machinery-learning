A model is not one clever step. It is the same two-part step (a *layer*) stacked dozens of times, 30 to over 120 layers in today's large models:

- **Look around** (attention, the previous chapter): each word gathers context from earlier words.
- **Think** (engineers call this the MLP or feed-forward layer): each word, on its own, works through what it now knows. Much of the model's stored knowledge seems to live here.

Each layer refines the understanding a little further. Researchers have found that, roughly speaking, early layers sort out words and grammar, middle layers work with ideas and relationships ("this is a bug report", "the user is frustrated"), and late layers turn all of that into a concrete guess about the next piece of text. At the very end, the model produces a score for every possible next token.

## What "70 billion parameters" means

The <Term k="params">parameters</Term> are the model's dials: the numbers inside all those layers that training adjusted, one tiny nudge at a time. "A 70B model" has 70 billion of them. They encode everything the model learned. The training data stops at some date, and the dials don't change afterwards. That's why models have a knowledge cutoff.

## The trick that keeps giant models affordable

Bigger models know more, but running every dial for every word is expensive. So most leading open-weight models, and at least some closed ones such as Google's Gemini, now use a <Term k="moe">Mixture of Experts</Term>: the "think" part is split into dozens or hundreds of specialists, and a small router picks a handful of them for each word, so only about 5% of the model works on any one word. <Claim id="deepseek-moe" cap />.

<Note title="Like a large consultancy">
The firm employs hundreds of experts, but each question only goes to a few of them, chosen by a router. The firm's total knowledge is huge; the cost of answering any single question stays modest. Unlike a real firm, the "experts" aren't the tax person or the lawyer: their specialisms are statistical, and nobody designed them.
</Note>

<Engineer title="Residual stream, superposition and reading a model card">
Each token's vector travels down the <Term k="residual">residual stream</Term>: every layer reads it and adds its contribution (x = x + layer(x)), like processes writing to a shared bus. The last position's final vector is multiplied by the unembedding matrix to give the <Term k="logits">logits</Term>, one score per vocabulary token. Most parameters sit in the <Term k="mlp">MLPs</Term> (about 80% in a dense model like Llama-3-70B, more in MoE models), most of the rest in the attention projections.

**Superposition.** A model with an 8,192-wide residual stream represents far more than 8,192 concepts, by packing them into nearly orthogonal directions and accepting some interference. This <Term k="superposition">superposition</Term> is why single neurons are usually polysemantic, and why researchers use sparse autoencoders to separate the vectors into millions of interpretable features.

**Reading a model card.** "70B parameters, 80 layers, hidden size 8192, 64 attention heads, 8 KV heads, 128k context, RoPE, SwiGLU, RMSNorm" decodes as: 80 blocks; a residual stream 8,192 floats wide; 64 query heads sharing 8 key/value heads (<Term k="gqa">GQA</Term>, to shrink the <Term k="kv">KV cache</Term>); a 128k-token window (that arrived with Llama 3.1; the original Llama 3 had 8k); rotary positions; and standard choices for the MLP non-linearity and normalisation.

**MoE in practice.** The router is a small learned layer that scores experts per token; usually the top 2 to 8 run. Early designs such as Mixtral used 2 of 8 experts; current ones use many small experts, so DeepSeek-V3, Llama 4 Maverick and Qwen3.5 all activate only about 4–6% of their parameters per token. All experts must sit in memory, so MoE saves compute, not RAM. Load-balancing losses keep the router from sending everything to a few favourites.
</Engineer>
