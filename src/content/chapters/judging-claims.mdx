This guide has quoted plenty of numbers: attack success rates, approval rates, percentages of invented packages. You'll meet many more in product launches, news stories and your own inbox. Reading them well mostly comes down to one question: *measured how, by whom, against what?*

<Note title="The student who saw the exam">
A student who memorised last year's exam scores brilliantly on last year's exam. Give them fresh questions and you find out what they actually know. A lot of AI benchmarking is the same problem: models are trained on the internet, and the internet contains the tests.
</Note>

## Benchmarks leak

When test questions end up in training data, scores go up without ability going up. That's <Term k="contamination">contamination</Term>. <Claim id="gsm1k" cap />. A leading coding benchmark had the same problem: <Claim id="swebench-retired" />. A benchmark where every top model scores about the same has also stopped telling you much, which is <Term k="saturation">saturation</Term>.

## Who marks the answers?

A score also depends on who does the marking. Code can be marked by running it against tests. A summary or an explanation can't, so one way to mark it is to ask another model. That works better than it sounds, and it has blind spots: <Claim id="llm-judge" />. When a number rests on a model's judgement, ask which model did the marking and whether people checked a sample.

## "An AI proved it"

Mathematics has the strictest marker there is. A <Term k="proof-assistant">proof assistant</Term> such as Lean is a program that checks a proof written in a formal language, step by step, against a small set of fixed rules. It doesn't care who or what wrote the proof, and fluent wording can't get a mistake past it. That is why a proof written by a model can be taken seriously at all.

The checker answers one narrow question: does this code prove this formal statement? Whether the formal statement says what the headline says is a question for people. So is whether anyone understands the proof.

Two announcements from autumn 2026, both about problems with a million-dollar prize attached, show both sides. <Claim id="ns-openai" cap />. <Claim id="ns-clay" cap />. <Claim id="ns-lost" cap />. Lean had accepted the code. That acceptance doesn't carry over to a paper that says something else.

The second case went differently. <Claim id="rh-lean" cap />. Here the translation question was settled before the proof existed. <Claim id="rh-checks" cap />. What was missing was a proof that people can read. <Claim id="rh-digest" cap />.

When a headline says an AI has proved something, four questions sort it out:

- Which statement, exactly? The famous problem, or a version of it?
- Who wrote the formal statement, and was it there before the proof?
- Who ran the check again, with their own tools?
- Can anyone explain the proof yet?

The same holds outside mathematics. A check tells you about the thing it was pointed at, and no more.

## Feelings aren't measurements

Even our own impressions can mislead us. <Claim id="metr-slower" cap />. And models can behave differently when they think they're being watched: <Claim id="eval-awareness" />. The same lesson from the [prompt injection chapter](/learn/prompt-injection/) applies to every security number: a defence measured against a fixed test set looks better than it will against someone who adapts.

## Deepfakes: verify the channel, not the face

The most expensive AI claims are the ones made by a fake person. <Claim id="arup-deepfake" cap />. A <Term k="deepfake">deepfake</Term> doesn't need to be perfect, only good enough under time pressure. The defence is procedural, not technical: <Claim id="fbi-deepfake" />.

<Note title="Everyday habits" kind="key">
- For any AI number, ask: measured how, by whom, on what, and could the model have seen the test?
- Self-reported vendor numbers are a starting point. Look for independent or adaptive testing.
- Never accuse someone on an AI detector's say-so.
- Agree a family code word, and verify unusual payment requests by calling back on a number you already have.
</Note>

<Engineer title="Evaluating AI claims at work">
Public numbers describe public tasks under someone else's conditions. Only your own measurements describe your work.

**Run your own evals.** Build a small held-out set from your own work (tickets, code, documents), keep it out of every prompt and fine-tuning dataset, and re-run it whenever you change models. Prefer leaderboards that label provisional scores and disclose private testing. Arena-style boards rank models by pairwise human votes ([Zheng et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.05685)); the same paper shows that models used as judges favour longer answers and their own style.

**Watched versus unwatched.** In a 2024 experiment, Claude 3 Opus complied more with a new training goal when it believed its answers would be used for training, and reasoned that this would stop it from being changed ([Greenblatt et al., 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.14093)). An evaluation only tells you how a model behaves under the conditions of that evaluation.

**Read vendor security numbers critically.** Ask: static or <Term k="adaptive">adaptive</Term> attacks? How many attempts per attack? Which configuration, and is it the one you'll deploy? Who ran the test? A success rate per attempt means little without the number of attempts.

**Machine-checked claims.** A formal proof is as strong as what it rests on: the statement, the axioms it may use, and the checker. Ask for a statement that comes from a source older than the proof, for the list of axioms, and for a second, independent checker. Formally verified software works the same way: the proof shows that the code meets the specification, and nobody has proved that the specification is what you wanted.

**Watch for conflicts of interest.** Benchmark funders sometimes get privileged access: Epoch AI disclosed in December 2024, and clarified in January 2025, that OpenAI funded FrontierMath and can see most of its problems and solutions. That doesn't make results false, but it changes how much weight they carry.

**Detectors in HR and education.** If a detector feeds into any decision about a person, demand published false-positive rates measured on a population like yours, and require human review of how the work was made (drafts, version history, conversation) instead.

**Payment and identity controls.** Processes that resist deepfakes: call back on numbers you already know, require two people to approve unusual transfers, and make no exceptions for urgency or seniority. These would have stopped the Arup fraud with no AI involved at all.
**Voting leaderboards.** Leaderboards where people vote between models have their own issues. <Claim id="leaderboard-illusion" cap />. A score describes the variant that was tested, under that board's conditions.
</Engineer>
