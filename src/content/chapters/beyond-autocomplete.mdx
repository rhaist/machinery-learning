Every response you have ever had from an AI, including a coding agent's multi-file change, was produced one token at a time. Nothing else comes out of the model. And yet it plainly doesn't feel like your phone's autocomplete. Three separate things close that gap, and it helps to keep them apart.

## 1. To predict well, the model has to understand what it's predicting

"Predict the next word" describes how the model was *trained*, not what happens inside it. To predict the next line of a proof, a stack trace or a detective story across trillions of examples, the cheapest strategy is often to build internal pictures of what's going on: the state of the program, the structure of the argument, who the murderer probably is. How complete and reliable those pictures are is actively debated (see the box further down).

<Note title="An everyday comparison">
To finish someone else's sentences reliably, you have to understand what they're talking about. Do it for every topic ever written about, and you end up needing to understand a great deal.
</Note>

## 2. Training changed what the predictions are *for*

A model straight out of the reading stage predicts what text would typically come next on the internet. Ask it to fix a failing test, and it may well continue the GitHub issue thread instead. Later training changes the goal: in practice environments, the tokens that eventually lead to passing tests get rewarded, even when the payoff comes fifty steps later. The model ends up *behaving as if* it pursues outcomes, because that is what was rewarded. Same kind of model, same prompt, three stages of training:

## 3. The tool loop turns one prediction into a process

A coding tool runs a loop around the model. The model writes a request to use a tool ("read this file", "run the tests") and stops. Ordinary software, the <Term k="harness">harness</Term>, carries out the request and adds the result to the conversation. Then the same, unchanged model is asked again with everything so far. There's no agent "thinking" in the background between steps; all continuity lives in the text. Part 2 opens with [the agent loop](/learn/agent-loop/), stepping through a whole session call by call.

Put the three together: a model that builds rich internal pictures in order to predict well, trained to act as if it pursues outcomes, running in a loop with tools, tests and room to think. "Next-token prediction" is accurate in the same way that "a computer just executes instructions" is accurate: true, and also not the whole story.

## When it feels like autocomplete again

The illusion breaks in predictable ways. Each one traces back to a mechanism from earlier chapters, and the mechanism tells you what to do about it.

<AutocompleteTable />

<Note title="Working with it, knowing all this" kind="key">
Give it a way to check its work, such as tests, a type checker or a linter, because that is the setting it was trained in. Spend context on the right files, an example to copy and the constraints. On anything non-trivial, ask for a plan first, and keep sessions short and focused. Treat confidence as style: fluency is what the model is best at, and only tests tell you the code is correct.
</Note>

<Engineer title="Policies, scratchpads and the evidence from interpretability">
Formally, the model's output is always a probability distribution over the next token. What training changes is what that distribution means.

**From estimator to policy.** After pretraining, the distribution estimates what a typical document would say next. After reinforcement learning, it is a <Term k="policy">policy</Term>: a strategy optimised for outcomes, whose actions happen to be tokens. That is the biggest single reason modern coding models feel goal-directed.

**Why written reasoning buys thinking time.** One forward pass has a fixed depth, typically 30 to 120 layers, which caps how many steps of computation can go into a single token. But every token the model writes becomes input for all later passes. Written reasoning therefore works as a <Term k="scratchpad">scratchpad</Term>, and each token adds another full trip through the stack. Compare the longest chain of computation with and without it:

<SerialDepth />

That is why chain-of-thought prompting worked ([Wei et al., 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2201.11903)), why simply adding "Let's think step by step" helped ([Kojima et al., 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.11916)), and why reasoning models are trained to do it at length.

**The written reasoning isn't a transcript.** In Anthropic's tests, Claude 3.7 Sonnet mentioned a hint it had used only 25% of the time (DeepSeek-R1: 39%), and models exploiting reward hacks almost never said so. A paper from several labs argues that reading the chain of thought is still a valuable safety tool, but a fragile one.

**Evidence from inside.** Anthropic's attribution graphs on Claude 3.5 Haiku found an intermediate "Texas" feature in the Dallas → Austin prompt; patching it to "California" made the model answer Sacramento. In rhyming couplets, the rhyme word was chosen at the end of line one, before line two was written, and swapping it rewrote the line. Othello-GPT's board was first found with non-linear probes (Li and colleagues). Nanda, Lee and Wattenberg then showed it can be read off linearly as "mine versus theirs", and that editing it changes the predicted moves.

The counterweight: Vafa and colleagues (2024) show that models can pass world-model tests while their internal maps are incoherent. And the attribution-graph method captures only part of the computation.

**The agent loop at token level.** The pattern of alternating reasoning with tool calls was named ReAct ([Yao et al., 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.03629)), and Toolformer showed that models can learn when to call a tool ([Schick et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.04761)). Each step is a fresh call on the full transcript. A tool call is text in a fixed format that ends with a stop token. <Term k="constrained">Constrained decoding</Term> keeps the arguments valid, <Term k="pcache">prompt caching</Term> keeps resending cheap, and <Term k="contextrot">context rot</Term> and compaction are why long sessions degrade.
</Engineer>
