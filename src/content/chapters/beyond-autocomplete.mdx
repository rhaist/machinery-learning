Every response you have ever had from an AI, including a coding agent's multi-file change, was produced one token at a time. Nothing else comes out of the model. And yet it plainly doesn't feel like your phone's autocomplete. Three separate things close that gap, and it helps to keep them apart.

## 1. To predict well, the model has to understand what it's predicting

"Predict the next word" describes how the model was *trained*, not what happens inside it. To predict the next line of a proof, a stack trace or a detective story across trillions of examples, the cheapest strategy is often to build internal pictures of what's going on: the state of the program, the structure of the argument, who the murderer probably is. How complete and reliable those pictures are is actively debated (see the box further down).

To finish someone else's sentences reliably, you have to understand what they're talking about. Do it for every topic ever written about, and you end up needing to understand a great deal.
</Note>

## 2. Training changed what the predictions are *for*

A model straight out of the reading stage predicts what text would typically come next on the internet. Ask it to fix a failing test, and it may well continue the GitHub issue thread instead. Later training changes the goal: in practice environments, the tokens that eventually lead to passing tests get rewarded, even when the payoff comes fifty steps later. The model ends up *behaving as if* it pursues outcomes, because that is what was rewarded. Same kind of model, same prompt, three stages of training:

## 3. The tool loop turns one prediction into a process

A coding tool runs a loop around the model. The model writes a request to use a tool ("read this file", "run the tests") and stops. Ordinary software, the <Term k="harness">harness</Term>, carries out the request and adds the result to the conversation. Then the same, unchanged model is asked again with everything so far. There's no agent "thinking" in the background between steps; all continuity lives in the text. Step through a typical session:

Put the three together: a model that builds rich internal pictures in order to predict well, trained to act as if it pursues outcomes, running in a loop with tools, tests and room to think. "Next-token prediction" is accurate in the same way that "a computer just executes instructions" is accurate: true, and also not the whole story.

## When it feels like autocomplete again

The illusion breaks in predictable ways. Each one traces back to a mechanism from earlier chapters, and the mechanism tells you what to do about it.

<AutocompleteTable />

<Note title="Working with it, knowing all this" kind="key">
Give it a way to check its work, such as tests, a type checker or a linter, because that is the setting it was trained in. Spend context on the right files, an example to copy and the constraints. On anything non-trivial, ask for a plan first, and keep sessions short and focused. Treat confidence as style: fluency is what the model is best at, and only tests tell you the code is correct.
</Note>

<Engineer title="Policies, scratchpads and the evidence from interpretability">
**From estimator to policy.** Formally the output is always a distribution over the next token. After RL it is no longer an estimate of "what would a typical document say here" but a <Term k="policy">policy</Term>: a strategy optimised for outcomes whose actions happen to be tokens. That is the biggest single reason modern coding models feel goal-directed: they were optimised for outcomes.

**Why reasoning tokens matter.** One forward pass has fixed depth (maybe a hundred layers), which caps the serial computation that can go into a single token. Writing an intermediate step makes it input for every later pass: the visible reasoning is a <Term k="scratchpad">scratchpad</Term> that buys as many sequential steps as needed. That's why "think step by step" worked, and why reasoning models are trained to do it at length. But the written reasoning is not a faithful transcript: in Anthropic's tests, Claude 3.7 Sonnet mentioned a hint it had used only 25% of the time (DeepSeek-R1: 39%), and models exploiting reward hacks almost never said so. A multi-lab position paper argues reading the chain of thought is still a valuable, if fragile, safety tool.

**Interpretability evidence.** Attribution graphs on Claude 3.5 Haiku showed an intermediate "Texas" feature in the Dallas → Austin prompt (patching it to "California" yields Sacramento) and, in rhyming couplets, the rhyme word being selected at the end of line one before line two is written; swapping that planned word rewrote the line to fit. Othello-GPT's board representation was first found with non-linear probes (Li et al.); Nanda, Lee and Wattenberg then showed it is linearly decodable as "mine vs theirs", and interventions on it changed predicted moves causally. The counterweight: Vafa et al. (2024) show models can pass world-model tests with incoherent internal maps. The attribution-graph method itself captures only a fraction of the computation.

**The agent loop at the token level.** Each step is a fresh call on the full transcript. Tool calls are text in a fixed format ending at a stop token; <Term k="constrained">constrained decoding</Term> keeps arguments valid; <Term k="pcache">prompt caching</Term> keeps the resend cheap; <Term k="contextrot">context rot</Term> and compaction are why long sessions degrade.
</Engineer>
