Part 1 said a model doesn't learn from your conversation while you're having it. That's true: the weights are frozen. But your words don't vanish when the chat ends. Depending on which product you use and how it's set up, they may be stored, read by a reviewer, used to train the *next* model, held for a court case, or published by accident.

<Note title="Postcard, envelope, notebook">
Typing into a free consumer chatbot is a bit like writing a postcard. The postal service promises not to look for long, but some staff might, copies can be kept for legal reasons, and if you pin it to a noticeboard (a share link) anyone can read it. A business contract is a sealed envelope with a written promise. A model on your own laptop is your private notebook: nobody else reads it, but it's only as safe as the lock on your door.
</Note>

## Consumer and business are different products

The biggest difference is between free or personal plans and business ones, more than between providers. For example, <Claim id="openai-training-default" />. Anthropic made the choice explicit: <Claim id="anthropic-consumer" />. Google adds people to the loop: <Claim id="gemini-retention" />.

Deletion isn't always the end either. <Claim id="nyt-hold" cap />. A <Term k="legalhold">legal hold</Term> overrides the provider's normal promise. Only setups that never store your text, such as <Term k="zdr">zero data retention</Term> or a local model, avoid that.

## Can a model remember what it learned?

Even if nobody stores your chat, what about text that went *into* training? Models mostly learn patterns, but they also <Term k="memorization">memorise</Term> some rare or often-repeated text word for word, like a student who learned a few textbook pages by heart, phone numbers included.

That memory can be pulled back out. <Claim id="extraction-chatgpt" cap />. Practical rule: assume anything public on the web may be inside a model somewhere.

## Memory and share links

Two convenience features deserve their own warning. Memory lets an assistant keep facts about you across chats, which also makes injection persistent: <Claim id="cosnitch" />. And share links are publishing: <Claim id="share-links" />.

## What EU law says

If you're in Europe, two sets of rules matter. GDPR already applies to what providers do with personal data. The EU AI Act adds duties for model makers: <Claim id="eu-gpai" />. None of this changes what a chatbot does with your words today; your provider's terms and settings do.

## Local isn't automatically safe

Running a model yourself removes the provider from the picture. It moves the risk to you. <Claim id="ollama-exposed" cap />.

<Note title="Everyday habits" kind="key">
- Check your settings. Turn off "improve the model" if you don't want your chats used for training.
- Don't paste passwords, health records, client data or other people's personal details into a consumer chatbot. Use your employer's approved tool for work.
- A share link is publishing. Review old shared links and stored memories now and then.
- Use temporary or incognito chat modes for sensitive one-off questions.
</Note>

<Engineer title="Data governance for AI use">
Settle in contracts and settings what you would otherwise have to hope for.

**Contracts.** Use business or API terms with no training on your data, documented retention, zero data retention where needed, and EU data residency where offered. Put these terms into your records of processing and your DPIA (data protection impact assessment). Track provider changes: retention and training defaults changed at several major providers in 2025 and 2026, so give these claims short re-check intervals.

**Shadow AI.** Blocking consumer AI on company devices without offering an approved alternative mostly drives use onto personal phones. Provide the sanctioned tool first, then govern it.

**RAG and fine-tuning.** Put as little personal data in as possible. Keep access controls on retrieved documents: the retriever must not fetch anything the user couldn't open themselves. Test fine-tuned models for regurgitating training records, especially rare strings such as IDs and keys.

**Self-hosting.** Never expose model servers without authentication. Put them behind a reverse proxy or VPN, verify model files (previous chapter) and log access. Most local runtimes have no authentication, and many guides and container images expose them on all network interfaces.

**Regulatory register.** Keep a living list of dates:

- <Term k="gpai">GPAI</Term> duties since August 2025, with Commission enforcement from August 2026.
- Article 50 transparency duties, with a grace period for watermarking that ends on 2 December 2026.
- High-risk rules in December 2027 and August 2028.

The Omnibus is Regulation (EU) 2026/1744, published in the Official Journal on 24 July 2026 and in force since 27 July 2026.
**Memory poisoning isn't new.** <Claim id="spaiware" cap />.

**Long texts can be extracted too.** <Claim id="books-extraction" cap />.

**EU detail.** The European data protection board says whether a model counts as "anonymous" must be judged case by case. Italy's regulator fined OpenAI €15 million in 2024, though a Rome court annulled the fine in March 2026 because the case belonged with the Irish regulator. The AI Act's timetable moved this year: <Claim id="eu-omnibus" />.
