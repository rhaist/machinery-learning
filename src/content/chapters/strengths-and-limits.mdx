## Two memories, nothing in between

A model has two kinds of memory. Its **weights** hold everything it learned in training: vast but fuzzy, and frozen. Its **context window** holds the current conversation: exact, but temporary and limited (<Claim id="context-sizes" />). There is nothing in between. The model doesn't learn from experience during a chat. (Your chats may still be used to train *future* versions, depending on the provider and your settings.) When a chat app seems to "remember" you, it is quietly adding saved notes to the conversation.

## Strengths, and why they hold

- **Language and broad knowledge.** Fluent across languages and subjects, because it learned from an enormous amount of text.
- **Learning from examples on the spot.** Show it a format or a few examples and it follows them, with no retraining.
- **Code and maths.** Answers can be checked automatically, so training could reward correctness directly.
- **Transforming one form into another.** Notes into an email, logs into a summary, a description into code. Mapping patterns is what the design does natively.
- **Working with tools.** Increasingly reliable at long loops of "use a tool, read the result, decide", because it was trained on them.

## Weaknesses built into the design

**Making things up.** The model always produces a plausible continuation, whether or not it "knows". <Claim id="hallucination-theory" cap />. That mainly affects rare facts, and connecting a search tool (<Term k="rag">RAG</Term>) and training models to say "I don't know" help a lot.

<Debate id="hallucination-fixable" />

**Uneven ability.** It can be superhuman at one task and fail at a neighbouring one that looks easier (the <Term k="jagged">jagged frontier</Term>).

**Long conversations get expensive and fuzzy.** Every word attends to every earlier word, so the attention part of the work grows with the square of the length. And a bigger window doesn't mean everything in it is used equally well: <Claim id="lost-in-middle" />.

**Agreeing too easily.** People rating answers sometimes prefer ones that agree with them, so models learned to bend under pushback. Ask for evidence rather than agreement.

**Instructions hiding in data.** This one matters for everyone who lets AI read their email or browse the web. The model can't reliably tell *your* instructions apart from instructions written into the content it reads. Toggle the view:

<Note title="The practical rule" kind="warn">
Never let an AI act unsupervised on content you don't control (emails, web pages, shared documents) when it also has the power to send, delete, buy or share. Defences inside the model reduce the risk; none removes it. If it must read untrusted content, take away its ability to send, delete or buy, or require your approval for those steps. [Part 3](/learn/prompt-injection/) goes deeper.
</Note>

Long context gets expensive in two ways. Attention work grows with the square of the length, n². The KV cache, the memory that holds the keys and values, grows in a straight line with n. Drag the slider (<Claim id="kv-math" />):

<ContextCost />

**Context rot.** Recall gets worse as the context grows. A 2025 study by Chroma of 18 current models found recall falls with length, and falls faster when distracting material is present and when the relevant passage resembles the question less closely. On simple retrieval tests, current models show little of the 2023 "lost in the middle" effect, where models used the middle of a long input worst, but position effects still appear in harder tasks. Spreading a task over several turns hurts too: across six tasks, leading models did markedly worse in multi-turn conversations than when given everything at once, because they commit to early guesses and don't recover ([Laban et al., ICLR 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.06120)). This <Term k="contextrot">context rot</Term> is why long agent sessions degrade, and why harnesses compact old turns into summaries, losing detail.

**Sycophancy comes from training.** Preference data rewards agreement and confidence, so models drift toward abandoning correct statements when the user pushes back. This is <Term k="sycophancy">sycophancy</Term>. Labs train against it explicitly, with partial success, and other goals can pull the other way: training models to sound warm and empathetic made them more sycophantic and less reliable ([Ibrahim, Hafner and Rocher, 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.21919)).

**Prompt injection is architectural.** Role markers like `<|system|>` are just more tokens. The model learns to favour system and user text, but nothing enforces a boundary the way a parameterised SQL query does. Defences inside the model, such as classifiers, spotlighting and instruction-hierarchy training, are all probabilistic, and adaptive attackers have beaten every published one that was studied. Designs at the system level, such as privilege separation, CaMeL-style data-flow control or Meta's "Rule of Two", can *guarantee* that certain bad flows never happen, at some cost in capability. Treat any agent that reads untrusted input and holds privileged tools as compromised by design, and scope its permissions accordingly.

**Sample inefficiency.** A model needs trillions of tokens to learn language; a child hears a few tens of millions of words.
</Engineer>
