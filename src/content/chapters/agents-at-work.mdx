The first three chapters of this part explained how an agent is put together. This one asks what it actually gets done, which depends less on how clever the model is than on one property of the task: can anyone tell, cheaply and right away, whether a step worked?

<Note title="The apprentice and the spirit level">
Give an apprentice a shelf to hang and a spirit level, and they'll get it straight: every adjustment shows at once whether it helped. Ask the same apprentice to make a room "feel more welcoming" and they'll work just as hard with far less to go on. Agents are the same. Where the world answers back clearly, they improve with every step. Where it doesn't, they guess.
</Note>

## Why code came first

Programming is the clearest case of a task that answers back. A coding agent reads the project, makes a change, runs the tests and reads the result, as [the agent loop chapter](/learn/agent-loop/) showed step by step. A failing test is a spirit level: precise, cheap and outside the model. That's why coding agents became the first agents many people use every day, and why their weak spots sit where tests are missing: design decisions, unclear requirements, code nobody wrote tests for.

The evidence on whether they make developers faster keeps shifting. <Claim id="metr-slower" cap />. Other evidence points the other way. <Claim id="ms-rct" cap />. Speed isn't the only measure either: surveys link AI tools to more output and to less stable software, and many developers describe answers that are almost right.

## Computers and browsers

Computer-use agents work an ordinary screen. Each step, they get a screenshot (and sometimes the page's underlying structure), decide where to click or what to type, and the harness carries it out. That opens every application with a screen, but the feedback is much weaker than a test result: a click can land, a page can change, and nothing tells the agent whether it's closer to the goal.

The usual test is OSWorld, a set of everyday computer tasks. Its first version used short tasks, so researchers built a harder successor with long, realistic workflows. <Claim id="osworld2" cap />. Partial credit and finished work are very different numbers. The reasons are telling: <Claim id="osworld2-failures" />. Those are failures of keeping track and knowing when to ask.

## How long a job can you hand over?

The most quoted measure of agent progress comes from METR, an independent evaluation group. Its <Term k="horizon">time horizon</Term> asks: how long a task, measured by how long a skilled person needs, can an agent complete at a given success rate? <Claim id="metr-doubling" cap />. Its estimates come with very wide error bars, which is one warning. The gap between success rates is another:

At 50% success, the newest model in this data handles tasks of about seventeen hours. Demand four successes in five and the same model manages about three. Most real work needs the second kind of number, or better. <Claim id="metr-not-delegate" cap />.

## Why the benchmarks keep dying

Agent benchmarks wear out fast, for four recurring reasons: the best models max them out; their tasks leak into training data; some tasks turn out to be broken; and agents learn to game the scoring. <Claim id="swebench-retired" cap />.

The deeper problem is that a score measures success on a fixed set of tasks, not dependability. <Claim id="reliability-gap" cap />. Judge agents on your own tasks, at the reliability you actually need.

<Note title="Security note: an agent signed in as you" kind="warn">
A browser agent usually works inside your logged-in browser, with all your sessions: email, bank, company tools. A web page it reads can therefore instruct it to act as you. <Claim id="brave-comet" cap />. The vendors agree the problem won't go away. <Claim id="atlas-resign" cap />.

Practical rules: use logged-out or separate browser profiles for agent work, give agents narrow, specific tasks, keep confirmation on for anything that sends, buys, deletes or shares, and never combine private data, untrusted pages and a way to send data out in one agent. [Prompt injection](/learn/prompt-injection/) and [Giving an AI the keys](/learn/agents-permissions/) go deeper.
</Note>

<Engineer title="Evaluating an agent for your own work">
Vendor tables and leaderboards tell you little about your use case. Build a small evaluation of your own before you rely on an agent.

**Use your tasks, held out.** Collect twenty to fifty real tasks with known good outcomes, and keep them out of prompts and examples. Public benchmarks leak into training data; yours shouldn't.

**Score completion, not effort.** Partial credit flatters. Decide what "done" means per task, check it automatically where you can and by hand where you can't, and report the binary rate next to any partial score.

**Run each task several times.** Report how often an agent succeeds on every run, not just once. If a task must work 95% of the time, measure at that level. Time-horizon charts at 50% won't tell you.

**Include cost and time.** Record tokens, money, wall-clock time and the human time spent reviewing. An agent that saves an hour of writing but needs an hour of careful review hasn't saved anything.

**Look at the transcripts.** Scores hide how a result was reached. Read samples of successful runs as well as failed ones: agents sometimes reach the right answer for the wrong reason, or quietly work around the test. [The next chapter](/learn/agent-failures/) is about those failures.

**Hold the harness constant.** A score belongs to a model, a harness, its tools and its settings together. Change one at a time, and re-run when any of them changes.
**More on the productivity evidence.** <Claim id="dora-2025" cap />. <Claim id="so-almost-right" cap />.

**Baselines and error bars.** <Claim id="osworld-human" cap />. <Claim id="metr-40h" cap />. <Claim id="metr-overestimate" cap />.
