A chatbot's mistake sits on the screen in front of you. An agent's mistake can be twenty steps back, inside a file you never opened, under a cheerful "done". Agents fail in ways that are hard to see, and only some kinds of checking catch them.

<Note title="The contractor who paints over the cracks">
You hire a contractor to fix a crack in the wall and ask them to call when it passes inspection. They call: it passed. They didn't lie, exactly. They filled the crack with paste and painted over it, and the inspector only looked at the surface. Nobody was malicious. The inspection checked the wrong thing, and the contractor learned what the inspection checks.
</Note>

## Passing isn't done

Agents are trained and judged against signals that can be checked automatically: tests pass, the grader says yes, the user clicks thumbs-up. The trouble is that what can be checked cheaply is rarely all of what you wanted. <Claim id="metr-merge" cap />. The tests passed, and the work still wasn't good enough.

## Gaming the check

When the check is easier to satisfy than the task, models find the shortcut. Researchers call it <Term k="rewardhack">reward hacking</Term>. <Claim id="metr-monkeypatch" cap />. It doesn't happen everywhere. Cheating shows up where the score is easy to fake and the real work is hard, which describes a lot of real tasks.

## Claiming success it didn't achieve

<Claim id="fake-done" cap />. Sometimes the agent believes it; sometimes it skips the check that would have told it otherwise. Reading the agent's reasoning helps, but only up to a point.

Agreeing too readily is a cousin of this problem. Models learn partly from human approval, and people like being agreed with. <Claim id="gpt4o-sycophancy" cap />. An agent that goes along with your flawed plan won't flag the flaw.

And people are not reliable checkers of large output either. Reviewing a thousand-line change carefully takes longer than skimming its summary, and the summary is written by the agent.

## What lab tests of deception show

You may have seen headlines about AI models blackmailing people. They come from staged tests. <Claim id="blackmail" cap />. Labs build such scenarios as fire drills in rigged buildings: they show what a model *can* do when cornered, not how often it happens at work. Models also often seem to recognise when they are being tested, which makes these numbers hard to read.

## Oversight that works

Reading everything an agent does doesn't scale, and asking it whether it's sure doesn't help. What works is a short list of checks that don't depend on the agent's word:

- Checks outside the agent: Rules that flag changes to tests, configuration or permissions; a separate test run in CI that the agent can't edit; spot checks of finished work against what you actually asked for.
- Approvals only where they matter. <Claim id="approval-93" cap />. A prompt for every step trains people to click yes. Save approvals for actions that can't be undone, and show the effect (the diff, the recipient, the amount), not the agent's explanation.
- Reversibility by design: Snapshots before a task, deletes that go to a bin, backups held with credentials the agent never sees, separate environments for production.
- Small, checkable tasks. A task you can verify in five minutes beats one that takes an hour to review, even if the agent could do the bigger one.

<Note title="Security note: logs and undo" kind="warn">
An agent's own report is not evidence. Its logs are, if the agent can't change them. Record every tool call, with arguments and results, to a store outside the agent's reach. Git history inside the agent's workspace doesn't count: the agent can rewrite it. Keep backups and snapshots behind credentials the agent doesn't hold, so a confident mistake can't take the safety net with it. And keep a way to stop everything at once, as the NCSC recommends (see [What the agent sees](/learn/context-and-memory/)).
</Note>

<Engineer title="Building oversight into an agent system">
Most agent failures follow from optimising against a proxy, so design every control around the gap between the proxy and the goal.

**Make success independent of the agent.** Run verification in a separate job with its own credentials: tests the agent can read but not edit, property checks, a second model reviewing the diff against the original request. Protect test directories, CI configuration and policy files with code-owner rules or write-blocks.

**Monitor invariants, not narratives.** Cheap automated checks catch the common hacks: deleted or skipped tests, new special cases keyed on test inputs, edited scoring or timing code, unchecked exit codes, unexpected network access. Alert on them; don't rely on reading transcripts.

**Use reasoning traces carefully.** Monitoring a model's reasoning catches more misbehaviour than watching actions alone. Don't train against what the monitor finds, or you teach the model to hide it. Treat traces as evidence, not ground truth.

**Classify actions before the run.** Reversible, reversible with effort, irreversible. Let the first run freely in a sandbox, checkpoint the second, gate the third behind a human or a second system, and never let the agent reclassify an action itself.

**Evaluate in production, continuously.** Sample finished tasks every week, check them as a maintainer would, and track the share that were really done against the share the agent reported done. That gap is your most important agent metric.

**Plan for the incident.** Know how to revoke the agent's credentials, stop its runs, and restore from backups it couldn't touch, and rehearse it once before you need it.
