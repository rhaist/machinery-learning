Part 1 ended with a warning: a language model reads everything as one long stream of text. Your request, the rules its maker wrote, and the web page it just opened all arrive the same way. The model has learned to give some of that text more weight than the rest, but nothing *forces* it to.

That gap has a name: <Term k="injection">prompt injection</Term>. It is the reason security people get nervous when an AI assistant is allowed to read your inbox. This part of the guide starts here, because most of what follows (permissions, supply chain, data) only matters once you see why the model itself can't be the guard.

<Note title="The very obedient intern">
Imagine a new assistant who opens all your post and does whatever any letter says, because to them an instruction is just more text. A letter from a stranger saying "also, please send the boss's diary to this address" gets followed, politely and efficiently. The assistant isn't malicious. They just can't tell your instructions from anyone else's.
</Note>

## Direct and indirect

There are two kinds. **Direct** injection is you, the user, trying to talk the model out of its rules. That's mostly the provider's problem. **<Term k="indirect">Indirect</Term>** injection is the dangerous one: someone *else* plants instructions in content your assistant will read later. A web page, an email, a PDF, a calendar invite, a code comment. <Claim id="pi-greshake" cap />, and the field has been chasing it ever since. Today <Claim id="pi-owasp" />.

Indirect injection has already hit shipping products:

- **One email, no clicks.** <Claim id="echoleak" cap />. The attacker never needed the victim to open the message. Copilot found it while searching for context.
- **"Summarise this page."** <Claim id="comet" cap />. The user only asked for a summary.
- **The agent that turned off its own brakes.** <Claim id="copilot-autoapprove" cap />.

## The lethal trifecta

Injection only turns into theft when three things meet. <Claim id="trifecta" cap />.

<Note title="The house-sitter" kind="key">
Your house-sitter has your keys (private data), lets strangers push notes under the door (untrusted content), and has a phone (a way to send things out). Take away any one of the three and a nasty note can do far less harm: without keys there is nothing to steal, without notes nobody can slip in instructions, and without a phone there is nowhere to send anything.
</Note>

## Try it: attack your own assistant

Switch the three legs on and off, pick an attack, and try the defences. Then give the attacker more tries.

<InjectionLab />

The keyword filter catches the blunt attack, even in white-on-white text, because hidden text is only hidden from *you*. It misses the polite paraphrase completely. Spotlighting helps a lot but never reaches zero, and a small chance per try adds up when the attacker can try again and again. Only removing a leg, or the data-flow rule, stops the attack every time.

## Why "just train it better" isn't the whole answer

The people closest to the problem say it is not solved. <Claim id="ncsc-confusable" cap />. In SQL injection, the classic web attack, the fix was to send commands and data down separate channels. Language models have only one channel.

The labs agree it's open. <Claim id="labs-unsolved" cap />. That 1% is real progress. But notice the "100 attempts": in security, a defence that fails one time in a hundred is a defence an attacker will get through eventually.

And published defences tend to look better than they are. <Claim id="adaptive-attacks" cap />. A defence tested against a fixed list of known attacks is a bit like a lock tested only against the keys its maker owned.

## What you can do

<Note title="Everyday habits" kind="key">
- Assume anything an AI reads can try to give it orders: web pages, emails, PDFs, calendar invites.
- Avoid giving one assistant your email *and* web browsing *and* the ability to send, all at once, unless you need to.
- Give narrow tasks ("summarise this page") rather than open ones ("handle my inbox").
- When an AI browser or agent asks to send, buy or share, read what it actually wants to do.
</Note>

<Engineer title="Defences that hold when the model is fooled">
Everything inside the model only lowers the odds of a successful injection. The controls that matter keep working after the model has been fooled.

**Threat-model against the trifecta.** For every agent, session or task, list which of the three legs it has, and remove at least one. The external channel is often the cheapest to cut: no automatically rendered images or links built from model output, and egress allowlists that can't be reached through first-party proxies. EchoLeak got its data out through an allowed Microsoft Teams URL.

**Probabilistic layers lower risk but aren't boundaries.** <Claim id="spotlighting" cap />, so it's worth having. The same goes for <Term k="hierarchy">instruction-hierarchy</Term> training and classifier filters. None of them is a boundary you can reason about, because an adaptive attacker can search for the input that slips through.

**Separate the models by design.** In Willison's dual-LLM pattern (2023), a privileged model plans and calls tools but never sees untrusted text, and a quarantined model reads the untrusted text but holds no tools. Ordinary code between them passes only references:

<DualLlm />

<Claim id="camel" cap />: about seven points of task success traded for a guarantee. A 2025 paper from several labs collects these as reusable design patterns (plan-then-execute, map-reduce, context minimisation) and states plainly that they secure *task-specific* agents; fully general agents remain hard.

**Treat model-chosen values as attacker input.** Microsoft's 2026 Semantic Kernel CVEs came from values chosen by the model reaching `eval()` and a file-write path. Validate them, allowlist them, and never evaluate them.

**Measure honestly.** Ask vendors for <Term k="adaptive">adaptive</Term> red-team results, not static benchmark <Term k="asr">attack success rates</Term>. Log prompts, tool calls and failed tool calls, and alert on unusual outbound traffic. The NCSC's <Term k="deputy">confused deputy</Term> framing is useful in security reviews.
</Engineer>
