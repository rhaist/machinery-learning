The rest of this guide explains how today's systems work. This page is about the order in which the pieces arrived. It's optional, and it's a simplification: every step below rests on decades of earlier work, and on faster chips and more data as much as on any single idea.

## 2012: neural networks start winning

A neural network is a program that learns from examples by adjusting millions of numbers. For decades it was one approach to AI among several, and not the favoured one. One result changed that: <Claim id="alexnet" />. What had changed was scale: more labelled images, more layers, and graphics chips to train on. Within a few years this approach, now called deep learning, was the default for images and speech, and then for text.

How learning by adjusting numbers works: [Training](/learn/training/).

## 2013 to 2016: learning by trial and error

Deep learning needs examples that come with the right answer. Reinforcement learning is a different way to learn: try something, get a score, adjust. The two are not rivals. One is a kind of model, the other a way to train it, and DeepMind, a London start-up that Google bought, put them together. <Claim id="dqn" cap />. Go was the harder test, because it has far too many positions to search through: <Claim id="alphago" />.

This strand goes quiet for a few years in the story of language models. It comes back in the last step.

## 2017: the transformer

Until then, networks read text one word after another, which made long texts slow to train on and easy to lose track of. <Claim id="transformer-2017" cap />. Because it handles a whole text in one go, it could be trained on far more text than anything before it. It was built for translation. Nobody was planning a chatbot.

How it works: [Attention](/learn/attention/) and [Inside the stack](/learn/inside-the-stack/).

## 2018 to 2020: read everything first

The next idea was to train a transformer on plain text with a simple exercise, such as guessing a hidden or the next word, and only afterwards adapt it to a job. <Claim id="bert-2018" cap />. Then the models grew, and something showed up that few had expected: <Claim id="gpt3" />. A big enough text predictor could be steered with words alone.

This is reading, the first of the three stages in [the opening chapter](/learn/start-here/). More in [Tokens](/learn/tokens/) and [Generating](/learn/generating/).

## 2022: a predictor becomes an assistant

A raw text predictor continues text. Ask it a question and it may reply with more questions. The missing step was the second stage, apprenticeship: show the model helpful conversations and train it on which answers people prefer. <Claim id="chatgpt-2022" cap />. Little about the technology was new that day. The packaging was, and it put the technology in front of the public.

More in [Training](/learn/training/).

## 2024 onwards: trial and error returns

Here the second strand comes back. Models now practise on tasks whose results can be checked automatically, such as maths with known answers or code with tests, much as AlphaGo practised by playing: <Claim id="reasoning-models" />. Models trained this way work through long problems more carefully and use tools more reliably, and a model that uses tools in a loop is what this guide calls an agent.

More in [Beyond autocomplete](/learn/beyond-autocomplete/) and [The agent loop](/learn/agent-loop/).

## What this story leaves out

- **Hardware and data.** None of these steps would have worked without graphics chips and without text and images at the scale of the internet.
- **Everyone else.** Each "first" above built on earlier work by many groups, and neural networks themselves go back to the middle of the last century.
- **The roads not taken.** This is the story as told from where the field stands now. Other approaches lost out, for the time being, and [Where research is heading](/learn/frontier/) lists ideas that may yet replace parts of today's design.
