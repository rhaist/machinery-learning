This is the first edition of the field notes. Each month they answer one question: **what changed in the field that should change how you understand or use these systems?** Most months, the honest answer will be "less than the headlines suggest".

## The big one: decision models

Since September, a kind of model that doesn't write text at all has taken off. TypeSafe AI released **Jev** in mid-September (it calls it a "System One" model). On 1 October AWS's Strands Labs released **Strands Decider 2B** and Cloudflare released **Clef**, both with open weights under the Apache 2.0 licence. Earlier open examples such as Laya existed, but this autumn is when the idea went mainstream. All of them take a situation plus a narrow question with fixed answers ("allow, ask a human, or block?") and return a probability for every option in a single pass, in tens to hundreds of milliseconds.

Under the hood this is less exotic than it sounds. Strands Decider starts from an ordinary pretrained language model (Qwen3.5-2B) and replaces the very last layer, the one that normally scores every possible next word, with a small layer that scores just the options you pass in, and lightly fine-tunes the rest. Everything the model learned about language stays; it simply can't ramble any more. The trick itself goes back to the reward models used to train ChatGPT's predecessors in 2022.

**Why it matters:** agent systems are full of small yes/no and pick-one decisions: is this input suspicious, may this tool run, is the task finished, which team should get this ticket? Often, each one meant a full call to a big model, or a custom classifier someone had to build. Decision models make those checkpoints cheap enough to put everywhere. The guide's new chapter [Around the model](../../learn/around-the-model/) has a live demo of one acting as a safety gate.

**Why to stay sceptical:** every headline benchmark so far comes from the makers. Strands reports 0.78 on JevBench for its current release (0.72 for its launch build), and Cloudflare reports Clef ahead of Jev; nobody has independently replicated either yet. One study, reported by Amplitude, found a decision model agreed with a frontier model's answers, mistakes included, 6–8 points more often than with the correct ones. Another found Jev answering "cheap" for 99.5% of model-routing requests, which is no better than always giving the same answer. A fast probability is only useful if it's calibrated. Measure on your own decisions before trusting one.

## The first frontier radar

The [radar](../../radar/) now tracks 22 directions across architecture, training, systems and security. This month's placements are the baseline. From next month on, you'll be able to replay how entries move.

Two placements worth explaining:

- **Diffusion language models: emerging.** A 2025 survey (revised in 2026) reports several-fold speed-ups at comparable quality, but a 2026 study found they currently hallucinate more than comparable autoregressive models.
- **Prompt-injection defences: emerging, not shipping.** Real mitigations exist and ship in products, but none solves the underlying problem that instructions and data share one channel. Calling them "shipping" would overstate the safety you get.

## What we're watching

- **Test-time training** (models that learn while they read): two strong papers in the past year, one an ICLR 2026 oral. It's the direction most likely to change the "two memories" picture in this guide.
- **Whether decision models hold up** under independent evaluation, and whether open rebuilds close the gap to Jev.
