This is the first edition of the field notes. Each month they answer one question: **what changed in the field that should change how you understand or use these systems?** Most months, the honest answer will be "less than the headlines suggest".

## Decision models

Since September, a kind of model that doesn't write text at all has taken off. TypeSafe AI released Jev on 15 September (it calls it a "System One" model). Convai Innovations followed with the open Laya three days later, and on 1 October AWS's Strands Labs released Strands Decider 2B and Cloudflare released Clef, both with open weights under the Apache 2.0 licence. All of them take a situation plus a narrow question with fixed answers ("allow, ask a human, or block?") and return a probability for every option in a single pass, in tens to hundreds of milliseconds.

Under the hood this is less exotic than it sounds. Strands Decider starts from an ordinary pretrained language model (Qwen3.5-2B) and replaces the very last layer, the one that normally scores every possible next word, with a small layer that scores just the options you pass in, and lightly fine-tunes the rest. Everything the model learned about language stays, but it no longer writes text. The technique is older than the name: GPT-1 and BERT (2018) already put small task heads on pretrained transformers.

Agent systems are full of small yes/no and pick-one decisions: is this input suspicious, may this tool run, is the task finished, which team should get this ticket? Often, each one meant a full call to a big model, or a custom classifier someone had to build. Decision models make those checkpoints cheap enough to put everywhere. The guide's new chapter [Around the model](../../learn/around-the-model/) has a live demo of one acting as a safety gate.

There are good reasons to stay sceptical. Strands reports 0.78 on JevBench for its current release (0.72 for its launch build), and Cloudflare reports Clef ahead of Jev; both are self-reported, and nobody has published a re-run of the Strands score. The first independent tests, mostly of Jev, are small and mixed: Vals AI found it matched frontier models on checking claims against company filings but ranked last of twelve on a slice of LegalBench. A study by AY Automate found a decision model agreed with a frontier model's answers, mistakes included, 6–8 points more often than with the correct ones. Another found Jev answering "cheap" for 99.5% of model-routing requests, which is no better than always giving the same answer. A fast probability is only useful if it's calibrated. Measure on your own decisions before trusting one.

## The patch wave

From July on, the number of published vulnerabilities jumped. In the public NVD and CISA data, critical CVEs published from August to October came to about three times the figure for the same months of 2025, while vulnerabilities newly confirmed as exploited rose much less, by under double. Vendors put the surge down to AI-assisted bug finding: Microsoft's October update alone fixed 1,413 flaws, more than in all of 2025. Finding flaws got cheap and fixing them didn't, so the bottleneck for security teams is now patching, which runs at the speed of their asset inventories, legacy systems and change windows. The new chapter [The patch gap](../../learn/patch-gap/) charts the data month by month and refreshes it with each update.

## The first frontier radar

The [radar](../../radar/) now tracks 22 directions across architecture, training, systems and security. This month's placements are the baseline. From next month on, you'll be able to replay how entries move.

Two placements worth explaining:

- **Diffusion language models: emerging.** A 2025 survey (revised in 2026) reports several-fold speed-ups at comparable quality, but a 2026 study found they currently hallucinate more than comparable autoregressive models.
- **Prompt-injection defences: shipping, not solved.** Classifiers such as Microsoft's Prompt Shields and instruction-hierarchy training are in mainstream products, so by the radar's own definition they are shipping. None of them solves the underlying problem that instructions and data share one channel, and adaptive attackers still get through.

## What we're watching

- **Test-time training** (models that learn while they read): two strong papers in the past year, one an ICLR 2026 oral. It's the direction most likely to change the "two memories" picture in this guide.
- **Whether decision models hold up** under independent evaluation, and whether open rebuilds close the gap to Jev.
- **Agent time horizons.** METR's 20 November update says the task length frontier agents finish half the time now doubles about every 3.6 months, and also warns that its own tests may overestimate real-world ability. The numbers at high reliability matter more for real use, and they lag far behind.
